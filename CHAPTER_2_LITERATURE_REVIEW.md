# CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter presents a comprehensive review of existing research and commercial solutions related to the development of a low-cost, offline voice assistant system for hospitality services. The literature is organised into five thematic areas: voice assistants in the hospitality industry, offline and edge-based speech recognition systems, small-scale natural language understanding (NLU) models for resource-constrained devices, privacy-preserving artificial intelligence in service industries, and task-oriented dialogue systems for domain-specific applications. The chapter concludes with an identification of research gaps that this study aims to address.

## 2.2 Voice Assistants in the Hospitality Industry

### 2.2.1 Adoption and Guest Experience

The integration of voice-based artificial intelligence (AI) assistants into hotel operations has emerged as a significant area of research and commercial interest in recent years. Buhalis and Moldavska (2021) conducted a foundational qualitative study examining in-room voice-based AI digital assistants and their impact on hotel services and guest experiences. Through 28 semi-structured interviews with both hospitality technology providers and hotel guests, the researchers found that the perceived benefits of voice assistants in hotels, including convenience, accessibility, and service speed, outweigh the drawbacks for both hotels and guests. However, the study identified privacy and usability as persistent concerns among guests, with many participants expressing discomfort regarding always-on microphones in private hotel rooms. Notably, this study focused exclusively on cloud-based commercial solutions such as Amazon Alexa and Google Assistant, without considering the potential of offline or low-cost alternatives that could address these privacy concerns.

Building upon their earlier work, Buhalis and Moldavska (2022) published a comprehensive analysis of voice assistant-enabled interactions between hotels and guests in the Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Technology. This study documented a broad range of use cases for voice assistants in hospitality settings, including room environment controls (lighting, temperature), room service ordering, wake-up alarms, and general information queries. The authors highlighted that voice assistants have the potential to streamline hotel operations by reducing the burden on front desk staff and enabling guests to access services without the friction of telephone calls or physical visits to the reception. However, the study remained limited to cloud-dependent platforms and did not present a technical architecture or cost analysis relevant to hotels in developing economies where cloud subscription costs and internet reliability are significant barriers.

The impact of voice assistant technology on hotel employees was investigated by Hwang and Erdem (2025) in a case study published in the Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Cases. Their research examined how voice assistant deployments transform the work environments of frontline hotel staff, arguing that the "U" in user experience extends beyond guests to include employees who interact with the technology daily. The study found that while voice assistants can reduce repetitive tasks and improve response times, staff members require adequate training and ongoing support to effectively integrate these tools into their workflows. This employee-focused perspective is relevant to the present research, as the proposed system includes a staff dashboard component designed to complement rather than replace human service delivery.

More recently, a 2025 study published in the Journal of Theoretical and Applied Electronic Commerce Research explored the relationship between voice assistant attributes and hotel reputation. Using structural equation modelling on 529 survey responses, the researchers examined how voice assistant characteristics, including connectivity, information association, and interactivity, influence positive customer evaluations and, consequently, online hotel reputation. The findings suggest that the interactive and informational capabilities of voice assistants contribute positively to guest perceptions, reinforcing the potential value of deploying such systems in hospitality environments.

### 2.2.2 Commercial Deployments

Amazon's Alexa for Hospitality, now branded as Alexa Smart Properties, represents the most widely deployed commercial voice assistant solution in the hotel industry. Launched in 2018, the platform enables hotels to deploy Amazon Echo devices in guest rooms with customised skills for room service ordering, housekeeping requests, facility information, and room controls. The system provides property-level device management, custom skill creation, and privacy features including the option to disable voice recording storage and physical microphone disconnect buttons. Major hotel chains including Marriott International, Hilton, and Best Western have piloted or deployed Alexa-powered devices across select properties (Marriott International, 2018).

Despite its market dominance, Alexa for Hospitality presents several significant limitations for the context of this research. First, the system requires constant cloud connectivity to Amazon Web Services (AWS) for all speech processing, rendering it non-functional during internet outages, a common occurrence in rural tourism areas of Sri Lanka. Second, the proprietary Echo hardware and AWS subscription model result in ongoing per-room costs that are prohibitive for budget and mid-range hotels in developing economies. Third, research has documented approximately 30% accuracy degradation when processing non-native English accents, which is particularly relevant for hotels serving guests with South Asian English pronunciation patterns.

### 2.2.3 Privacy Concerns in Hotel Voice Technology

The deployment of always-on voice assistants in private hotel rooms has raised significant privacy concerns. Research conducted at Purdue University in 2018 identified specific privacy vulnerabilities in hotel Alexa deployments, including the potential for malicious third-party skills to eavesdrop on guest conversations through the Alexa Skills Kit. The study documented scenarios where guests might unknowingly disclose sensitive information, including credit card numbers, travel plans, or personal conversations, in the presence of cloud-connected listening devices. While Amazon has subsequently implemented additional privacy controls, the fundamental architectural issue remains: all voice data is transmitted to and processed on external cloud servers, creating an inherent privacy risk that cannot be eliminated within a cloud-dependent architecture. This finding provides a strong motivation for the edge-computing approach adopted in the present research, where all voice processing occurs locally within the hotel's network.

## 2.3 Offline and Edge-Based Speech Recognition Systems

### 2.3.1 The Vosk Speech Recognition Framework

Vosk, developed by Alpha Cephei, is an open-source offline speech recognition toolkit that supports multiple languages and operates without internet connectivity. The framework provides lightweight models ranging from 50MB to 1.8GB, enabling deployment on resource-constrained devices including mobile phones, Raspberry Pi boards, and embedded systems. Vosk supports 20+ languages and provides real-time speech recognition with word-level timestamps, making it suitable for applications requiring low-latency, privacy-preserving speech processing. The present research utilises the vosk-model-en-us-0.22-lgraph variant (205MB), which is optimised for mobile deployment while maintaining acceptable accuracy for English speech recognition.

Korkmaz et al. (2025) developed a prototype edge-based speech-to-text system using Vosk, demonstrating real-time, ultra-low latency communication with AI-powered natural language processing. Their system was deployed on constrained hardware including Raspberry Pi devices, achieving practical speech recognition performance without cloud connectivity. The research validated that edge-based STT using Vosk can deliver latency comparable to cloud solutions for short utterances typical of command-and-control applications. However, their work focused on general-purpose communication rather than domain-specific applications such as hospitality, and did not integrate downstream NLU classification or service management components.

Research on improving Vosk's accuracy through custom language models has demonstrated significant potential for domain-specific optimisation. A 2025 study showed that integrating custom language models with the Vosk toolkit can achieve up to 40% Word Error Rate (WER) reduction compared to off-the-shelf models when processing domain-specific vocabulary. This finding is relevant to the present research, as hotel service requests contain specialised vocabulary (e.g., "housekeeping," "concierge," "amenities") that may benefit from similar language model customisation in future iterations of the system.

### 2.3.2 Edge-Based Speech Processing for IoT

Chantrapornchai and Suchato (2022) explored offline automatic speech recognition on edge devices for IoT control applications. Their research deployed ASR on Raspberry Pi hardware, benchmarking edge processing capabilities for spoken device commands. The study demonstrated that embedded devices can achieve practical speech recognition performance for simple command structures, though accuracy degrades with longer utterances and complex sentence structures. While their work validated the feasibility of edge-based speech recognition, it was limited to simple command recognition without a natural language understanding pipeline and was not applied to service industry contexts where request classification and routing are required.

A comprehensive evaluation of voice recognition platforms and methods for edge AI devices was conducted in 2024, benchmarking multiple ASR frameworks including Vosk, Whisper, and proprietary solutions across dimensions of accuracy, latency, and resource consumption. The study found that Vosk provides the best balance of accuracy and resource efficiency for real-time edge deployment, while Whisper achieves higher accuracy but requires significantly more computational resources. This trade-off between accuracy and resource consumption is central to the architectural decisions in the present research, where on-device deployment on commodity Android tablets constrains the available computational resources.

### 2.3.3 Large-Scale Speech Recognition Models

Radford et al. (2023) introduced Whisper, a large-scale speech recognition model trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual and multitask supervised data collected from the web. Whisper achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across 99 languages and demonstrates remarkable robustness against background noise and speaker accents. The model is available in multiple sizes, from "tiny" (39M parameters) to "large" (1.55B parameters), with accuracy scaling proportionally with model size. While Whisper represents the current accuracy ceiling for speech recognition, its computational requirements, particularly for the medium and large variants, make it unsuitable for on-device deployment on commodity mobile hardware. The smallest Whisper variants (tiny, base) can run on mobile devices but with significantly reduced accuracy. In the present research, Whisper is utilised as a server-side benchmark for evaluating the accuracy trade-off of the on-device Vosk deployment.

## 2.4 Small-Scale NLU Models for Resource-Constrained Devices

### 2.4.1 BERT and the Transformer Revolution

The introduction of BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) by Devlin et al. (2019) fundamentally transformed the landscape of natural language understanding. BERT's bidirectional pre-training approach, which considers both left and right context simultaneously, achieved state-of-the-art results across a wide range of NLU benchmarks. However, the original BERT-BASE model, with 110 million parameters and a size of approximately 440MB, is too large for practical deployment on mobile devices. This limitation has motivated significant research into model compression techniques, including knowledge distillation, quantisation, and architectural optimisation, to create smaller models suitable for edge deployment.

### 2.4.2 DistilBERT: Knowledge Distillation for NLU

Sanh et al. (2019) introduced DistilBERT, a distilled version of BERT that retains 97% of BERT's language understanding capabilities while being 40% smaller in size and 60% faster in inference speed. DistilBERT employs knowledge distillation during pre-training, using the original BERT model as a teacher to train a smaller student model with 6 transformer layers (compared to BERT's 12). The resulting model achieves strong performance on the GLUE benchmark with significantly reduced computational requirements. While DistilBERT represents a meaningful step towards mobile NLU, it remains larger than models specifically optimised for mobile inference, such as MobileBERT, and has not been extensively evaluated for on-device deployment on Android devices in domain-specific applications.

### 2.4.3 MobileBERT: Purpose-Built for Mobile Deployment

Sun et al. (2020) proposed MobileBERT, a compact, task-agnostic BERT model specifically designed for resource-limited devices. Through a progressive knowledge transfer strategy from an inverted-bottleneck BERT-LARGE teacher model, MobileBERT achieves 4.3x model compression and 5.5x inference speedup over BERT-BASE while achieving a GLUE benchmark score of 77.7, only 0.6 points lower than BERT-BASE. On a Google Pixel 4 smartphone, MobileBERT achieves an inference latency of 62 milliseconds, making it practical for real-time on-device natural language understanding. The present research adopts MobileBERT as the foundation for its intent classification pipeline, fine-tuning the model on a custom hotel service dataset and converting it to TensorFlow Lite format for deployment on commodity Android tablets. While Sun et al. evaluated MobileBERT on general NLP benchmarks, the present study provides the first documented evaluation of MobileBERT fine-tuned for hospitality-specific intent classification.

### 2.4.4 DIET: Server-Side Intent and Entity Recognition

Bunk et al. (2020) introduced DIET (Dual Intent and Entity Transformer) as part of the Rasa NLU framework. DIET is a multi-task architecture that handles both intent classification and entity recognition within a single model, outperforming fine-tuned BERT on several benchmarks while being six times faster to train. The architecture supports pluggable pre-trained embeddings, including BERT, GloVe, and ConveRT, allowing practitioners to balance accuracy and computational cost. While DIET represents a significant advancement in server-side NLU, it is designed as a component of the Rasa framework and requires server infrastructure for deployment. It is not optimised for on-device mobile inference, which is a core requirement of the present research. The Rasa framework's dependency on a running server process contrasts with the edge-computing approach adopted here, where NLU processing occurs entirely on the guest's Android device.

### 2.4.5 Domain-Specific Fine-Tuning for Intent Classification

Bujel et al. (2021) investigated the effectiveness of pre-training for few-shot intent classification, demonstrating that fine-tuning BERT with as few as 1,000 labelled examples can surpass existing pre-trained models for intent classification in novel domains. Their work on IntentBERT showed that domain-specific pre-training significantly improves classification accuracy when training data is limited, which is typically the case for specialised applications such as hotel service requests. However, the study focused on few-shot learning scenarios and did not address the deployment of fine-tuned models on mobile devices or edge hardware.

Additional research on domain-specific vocabulary extension for BERT-based intent recognition was published in Applied Sciences (2022). This study proposed a frequency cut-off strategy for extending BERT's vocabulary with domain-specific terms, demonstrating improved accuracy on specialised classification tasks. The approach is relevant to the present research, where hotel-specific vocabulary (e.g., "concierge," "housekeeping," "amenities," "wake-up call") may not be well-represented in the general-purpose pre-training corpus of MobileBERT.

## 2.5 Privacy-Preserving AI in Service Industries

### 2.5.1 Edge Computing Foundations

The theoretical foundation for edge computing was established by Shi et al. (2016) in their seminal paper "Edge Computing: Vision and Challenges," published in the IEEE Internet of Things Journal. The authors articulated a vision where data processing occurs at the network edge, close to data sources, rather than being transmitted to centralised cloud data centres. This paradigm offers three key advantages: reduced latency through local processing, improved privacy through data locality, and reduced bandwidth consumption. The edge computing vision is directly applicable to the present research, which processes all guest voice data on the local Android device and hotel server, eliminating the need for external data transmission.

### 2.5.2 On-Device AI Model Deployment

A comprehensive survey on on-device AI models published in ACM Computing Surveys (2025) examined the techniques and challenges of deploying machine learning models on resource-constrained devices. The survey covered model compression methods including quantisation (reducing numerical precision from 32-bit to 8-bit or lower), pruning (removing redundant model parameters), and knowledge distillation (training smaller models to mimic larger ones). The authors noted that TensorFlow Lite (TFLite) has emerged as the dominant framework for deploying neural networks on Android devices, supporting both CPU and GPU acceleration. The present research utilises TFLite for deploying the fine-tuned MobileBERT model, converting the PyTorch-trained model through the TensorFlow intermediary format to produce an optimised 26MB TFLite file suitable for on-device inference.

### 2.5.3 Privacy-Preserving Speech Recognition

Research on privacy-preserving on-device speech recognition using Vosk with domain-specific language models (2025) demonstrated that fully offline speech processing can achieve competitive accuracy compared to cloud-based alternatives. The study benchmarked custom Vosk models against Google Cloud Speech-to-Text and Microsoft Azure Speech Services, finding that domain-optimised Vosk models achieved a 40% WER reduction over generic offline models, approaching the accuracy of cloud solutions for domain-specific vocabularies. Critically, the study emphasised that on-device processing provides an architectural guarantee of privacy, as voice data never leaves the user's device, a claim that cannot be made by cloud-based systems regardless of their privacy policies. This architectural advantage is a core contribution of the present research, where all speech-to-text processing occurs on the guest's Android tablet within the hotel's local network.

## 2.6 Task-Oriented Dialogue Systems

### 2.6.1 Intent Classification and Slot Filling

Larson et al. (2022) conducted a comprehensive survey of intent classification and slot-filling datasets for task-oriented dialogue systems. The survey catalogued existing benchmarks including ATIS, SNIPS, and various domain-specific datasets, identifying a significant gap in the availability of datasets for specialised service domains. The authors noted that most publicly available datasets focus on general-purpose assistant tasks (e.g., music playback, weather queries) rather than industry-specific operations such as hospitality, healthcare, or logistics. This observation directly motivates the creation of a custom hotel service intent dataset in the present research, comprising 4,971 labelled examples across 18 hospitality-specific intent categories.

Research on improved spoken language representation for intent understanding in task-oriented dialogue systems was published in Sensors (2022). This study explored how acoustic features from speech can be combined with textual features to improve intent classification accuracy, particularly for spoken inputs where speech recognition errors may degrade text-based classification performance. The findings suggest that joint modelling of speech and text features can partially compensate for STT errors, an insight relevant to the present research where Vosk transcription errors may impact downstream intent classification accuracy.

### 2.6.2 Multi-Task and Efficient Dialogue Models

Recent research on multi-task dialogue models has focused on reducing computational requirements while maintaining performance. The AUTODIAL framework (2025) introduced parallel decoders for simultaneous intent prediction and dialogue state tracking, achieving 3-6x inference speedup with 11x fewer parameters compared to baseline models. While such efficiency gains are valuable, these models remain oriented towards server-side deployment and do not address the specific constraints of on-device mobile inference. Furthermore, these dialogue models focus on multi-turn conversational interactions, whereas the present research addresses single-turn voice commands typical of hotel room service requests, where the interaction pattern is: guest speaks request, system confirms, guest approves, and request is submitted.

## 2.7 Technology Adoption in Sri Lankan Tourism

### 2.7.1 Digital Readiness of Sri Lankan Hospitality

Research examining Sri Lanka's preparedness for digital tourism has highlighted both opportunities and challenges. A 2022 study published by Springer, "Connecting the Connected: How Is Sri Lanka Prepared to Respond to Digital Tourists?", analysed the technological capabilities of four major tourism operators: hoteliers, restaurateurs, airlines, and tour operators. The study found that while leading hotel chains in Sri Lanka have adopted basic digital technologies such as online booking systems and mobile applications, the adoption of advanced AI-powered guest services remains limited. Key barriers identified include the high cost of technology infrastructure, limited IT expertise in the hospitality workforce, unreliable internet connectivity in rural tourism areas, and a general risk-averse attitude towards technology investment among smaller hotel operators. These findings directly support the motivation for the present research, which aims to provide a low-cost, low-complexity solution that overcomes these specific barriers.

### 2.7.2 Smart Tourism and Digital Transformation

A broader analysis of smart tourism in the digital age, published in Revista de Gestao (2024), examined the barriers and enablers of digital transformation in tourism across developing economies. The study identified cost, infrastructure, and digital literacy as the three primary barriers to technology adoption in hospitality. The authors argued that solutions designed for developing economies must prioritise affordability, offline capability, and ease of deployment to achieve meaningful adoption. Sri Lanka's tourism sector, which is projected to generate USD 8 billion in revenue by 2028, stands to benefit significantly from technology solutions that enhance guest experience and operational efficiency without requiring substantial capital investment. The present research directly addresses this need by proposing a system that operates on locally available commodity hardware with no recurring cloud costs.

The comparison matrix reveals that while individual components of the proposed system, specifically offline STT, compact NLU models, and hospitality voice assistants, exist in the literature, no existing work combines all seven dimensions into an integrated solution. The following five research gaps are identified:

### 2.8.1 Gap 1: No Integrated End-to-End Offline Voice Assistant for Hospitality

Existing research addresses individual components in isolation: edge-based STT (Korkmaz et al., 2025; Chantrapornchai & Suchato, 2022), NLU model compression (Sun et al., 2020; Sanh et al., 2019), and hospitality VA adoption studies (Buhalis & Moldavska, 2021, 2022). However, no published work combines all pipeline components, namely speech-to-text, natural language understanding, text-to-speech, backend management, and staff notification, into a single integrated, offline-capable system specifically designed for hotel operations. Hotels in developing economies cannot afford to integrate multiple separate commercial solutions, and a unified system that operates on commodity hardware removes the need for specialised IT staff while reducing total cost of ownership.

### 2.8.2 Gap 2: Absence of On-Device NLU for Hospitality-Specific Intent Classification

While MobileBERT (Sun et al., 2020) and DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019) have been evaluated on general NLP benchmarks such as GLUE and SQuAD, and Rasa DIET (Bunk et al., 2020) provides server-side intent classification, no published research evaluates fine-tuned compact transformer models on hospitality-specific intent datasets deployed as TFLite models for on-device inference on commodity Android devices. Cloud-based NLU services such as Google Dialogflow, Microsoft LUIS, and Amazon Lex incur per-request API costs that scale with usage. Hotels processing hundreds of daily requests across dozens of rooms would face unsustainable recurring costs that on-device NLU eliminates entirely.

### 2.8.3 Gap 3: Privacy-Preserving Voice Processing in Hospitality Without Cloud Dependency

Research on privacy-preserving AI (Shi et al., 2016) establishes theoretical frameworks, and studies document specific privacy concerns with hotel Alexa deployments (Purdue University, 2018). However, no published work demonstrates a practical, privacy-preserving voice assistant system for hotels where all voice data is processed locally and never transmitted beyond the hotel's local network. As data protection regulations emerge across South and Southeast Asia, and hotels increasingly cater to European tourists with GDPR expectations, the need for privacy-compliant local processing solutions is urgent. The present system provides an architectural guarantee of privacy by design.

### 2.8.4 Gap 4: Real-Time Bidirectional Communication Between Guest Voice Devices and Staff Systems

Commercial solutions such as Alexa for Hospitality provide primarily one-directional service from guest to system. Academic research on hotel voice assistants (Buhalis & Moldavska, 2021, 2022) focuses on guest experience but does not address real-time staff notification, bidirectional messaging between staff and guests, automated department routing, or request lifecycle management integrated with voice devices. Hotels in developing economies often operate with complex department structures where a single guest request may require routing or escalation. Real-time bidirectional communication between guest devices and department-specific staff dashboards has the potential to eliminate the need for expensive PBX or intercom systems while reducing service response times.

### 2.8.5 Gap 5: Cost-Effective Voice AI Deployment Using Commodity Android Devices

Existing implementations of hotel voice assistants use either proprietary hardware, such as Amazon Echo devices for Alexa or Google Nest Hub, or specialised IoT devices such as Raspberry Pi with custom hardware configurations. No research demonstrates a production-viable voice assistant system running on standard, commercially available Android tablets that hotels can procure locally at minimal cost. Android tablets are widely available in Sri Lanka at price points of $50 to $150 USD, compared to $200 or more for smart speakers with limited local availability. Utilising commodity hardware that local technicians can service and replace reduces both initial deployment costs and ongoing maintenance expenses, a critical consideration for the Sri Lankan hospitality market.

## 2.9 Summary

This chapter has reviewed the existing body of research across five thematic areas relevant to the development of a low-cost, offline voice assistant system for hospitality services. The review reveals that while significant progress has been made in individual areas, including cloud-based hotel voice assistants, offline speech recognition, compact NLU models, and edge computing, no existing work integrates these components into a unified, offline-capable system designed for the hospitality domain in developing economies. Five specific research gaps have been identified, spanning the dimensions of end-to-end integration, on-device hospitality NLU, privacy preservation, bidirectional staff communication, and cost-effective commodity hardware deployment. The present research aims to address all five gaps through the design, development, and evaluation of an integrated voice assistant system that operates entirely within a hotel's local network using commodity Android devices. The following chapter presents the methodology adopted to achieve this objective.
